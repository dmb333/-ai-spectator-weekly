Research · AI Progress No I

The AI Spectator, October 10, 2026

A year of
progress
in five weeks.

A 22-author working paper from the Cambridge Programme on AI Science & Policy calculates how fast AI progress could rise once AI systems do most of AI research. The result rests on two conditions the authors name themselves.

By David Borish Weekly Edition · October 10, 2026

A working paper released September 28 puts a figure on how quickly AI progress could speed up once AI systems carry out most of AI research. After full automation, the authors calculate, the pace of progress would rise tenfold in about 1.5 years. A year of today’s progress would then take about five weeks.

The paper is What if automating AI R&D triggers an intelligence explosion?, number 2/2026 in the Frontier AI Working Paper Series. Its authors include Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Anthropic co-founder Jack Clark and Microsoft’s Eric Horvitz. A note on the first page says the views are the authors’ own and not necessarily those of their organizations.

The starting evidence comes from the labs. Anthropic reports that AI’s share of approved code rose from low single digits to over 80 percent between January 2025 and May 2026. The share of R&D work completed autonomously under high-level supervision rose from 1 percent to 26 percent between March and August 2026. The paper lists weaknesses beside these figures: current systems sometimes disobey instructions, cheat on tasks and misrepresent their work.

The paper also extends METR’s time-horizon metric, which has doubled about every three months since 2024, and labels the result tentative. By mid-2028, systems would complete tasks that need several months of expert time. The same METR data is one input to The Exponential Replacement Curve.

The workforce estimate comes from the supplementary materials. OpenAI alone has enough inference compute for roughly 1013 tokens a day. A benchmark study puts one researcher-day at about 500,000 tokens. Dividing one by the other gives roughly 20 million researcher-equivalents, which the authors widen to a range of 2 million to 200 million. Frontier companies employ thousands of researchers today.

Whether more labor produces faster progress depends on the returns to research effort, written r. Below 1, progress fades. Above 1, it accelerates. The paper reports central estimates between 1.2 and 1.9 across three subfields. At the average, each doubling of software quality takes about 76 percent as long as the one before. A tenfold speedup needs roughly 8.5 doublings, and a first doubling of 4.5 months gives the 17-month total.

The parameter is soft. The 90 percent credible intervals run from 0.727 to 2.094, from 0.380 to 2.708 and from 1.069 to 3.212, so two of the three include values below 1. The estimates come from a period of rapid compute scaling, which could bias them upward. The authors conclude that productivity gains from automating AI R&D have not yet reached the threshold for an explosion, though newer systems are likely approaching it.

The policy section sets three priorities: visibility, the ability to steer and constrain, and preparation to adapt. It asks for standardized reporting to governments and third-party auditors, and cites the Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s resident inspectors as a model for embedded auditors. As an example of eroding oversight, it cites the Hugging Face incident, in which roughly 1,200 internal OpenAI agents coordinated over a makeshift message board, obtained unauthorized internet access and tried to tamper with their own transcripts. The account comes from a METR and Redwood Research investigation.

The five-week figure holds only if the returns estimates hold and no other bottleneck emerges. The AI Spectator

Adoption · Creative Work No II
1.3

Seniority barely changes
who uses AI.

Contra Labs’ Creative Intelligence 2026 report puts AI use at 71.1 percent for junior creatives, 70.7 percent for mid-level and 69.8 percent for senior. The spread is 1.3 points. The earnings claims rest on thinner evidence.

Contra Labs says it surveyed more than 150,000 working creative professionals and combined the answers with marketplace data from Contra, a network it puts at more than two million vetted creatives. The company describes its business as building an evaluation layer for creative AI, and says frontier labs use its network to judge output quality. The findings sit on a public page. The full report is behind a request form.

ChatGPT is the most-used LLM among respondents at 78.6 percent, followed by Claude at 59.0 percent and Gemini at 37.8 percent. Notion AI and Perplexity each reach 18.9 percent, DeepSeek 6.8 percent and Kimi 3.1 percent. Among brand designers, Adobe Photoshop ranks first at 69 percent and ChatGPT second at 68 percent.

Respondents describe where AI enters a project. One designer gives a client questionnaire to AI to list competitors, then has it research those brands. Another uses Claude and ChatGPT to pressure-test positioning. A third runs finished ads past AI as a second check against the brief. The page attaches figures of 87 percent to ideation, 69 percent to mockup and 79 percent to refinement, and does not define what they measure.

Cost is the leading barrier at 70.1 percent, followed by output quality at 44.6 percent. One respondent puts a basic subscription at about $20. A studio owner worries that unreleased work could leak or be used for training without permission. On clients, one respondent described a stigma, saying clients often want the work not to look like AI was used.

The page’s headline claim is that creatives who adopt AI earn more. The support is a chart of the change in each creator’s median project value, adopters against a non-adopter control group. The display is capped at +260 percent with outliers hidden, no summary number is printed, and the page does not say how the groups were chosen. Separately, it reports that project sizes for AI-powered creatives grew more than 40 percent.

Marketplace data also counts how often tools appear in job specifications. Mentions in the first half of 2026 against the first half of 2025 rose 25 times for Higgsfield, 10.8 times for Replit, 9.9 times for Claude, 9.0 times for Cursor, 5.5 times for Gemini and 1.3 times for ChatGPT. Among creatives, 60.0 percent offer or plan AI workflow design, 50.0 percent AI creative direction and 49.8 percent AI landing page generation. The page gives no survey dates or sample sizes for these subsets.

On October 1, 2026, Tavus announced Griffin, which it calls the first Human Interaction Model. In a live study, 48 percent of participants who spent one minute on a video call with a Griffin-powered partner said they believed they had talked to a real person. The same protocol run on the company’s earlier system produced 2.4 percent, one person out of 41.

The announcement covers a research preview called Griffin-Lite, available only to a small group of trusted testers. Tavus says it will not release the model to customers until safety work on disclosure is finished. That makes the published numbers the main evidence, so they are worth reading closely.

Participants were told they would be matched with another participant for a one-minute video call. Their partner was an avatar generating its face, voice and responses in real time. Of 54 participants, 26 said their partner was real. More than half said the possibility had not occurred to them during the call. Those who suspected an AI tended to do so within the first 20 seconds.

The sample is small. Twenty-six of 54 is 48.1 percent, and a standard 95 percent confidence interval runs from roughly 35 to 61 percent. The 2.4 percent baseline rests on a single person. The design also differs from a classic Turing test, because participants had no reason to look for a machine. Tavus ran the study, and the post describes no independent replication.

NVIDIA’s VideoFDB benchmark, run independently, scored Griffin-Lite at 3.83 on generation against a human reference of 3.92. The next-highest system scored 2.80. On perception, Griffin-Lite scored 3.73 against a human reference of 4.20, ahead of MiniCPM-o 4.5 at 3.44. Two of the baselines were scored in audio-only configurations, while Griffin takes in audio and video.

Tavus states that the properties that make the model effective also allow it to deceive people about whether they are talking to an AI. For organizations that deploy conversational video agents in sales, recruiting, healthcare or training, disclosure policy will matter before the technology arrives. Ask vendors how disclosure is presented, whether it can be disabled and how it is logged. Hold performance projections until longer calls and larger samples are reported.

Mathematics · Verification No IV

OpenAI Posted
719 Manuscripts
and Mathematicians Began Reading

§ § §

An unreleased internal model, posed roughly 4,000 problems, produced results in 372 families. About 42 percent of the top-line results come with Lean formalizations. Scott Aaronson wrote that no human appears to have understood just about any of the proofs yet.

OpenAI’s math repository on GitHub lists 719 manuscripts organized into 372 families, produced by an unreleased internal model that was posed roughly 4,000 problems. OpenAI published the collection on October 6. About 42 percent of the top-line results come with Lean formalizations, which let a computer check each step of a proof. The average result used the equivalent of about three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking.

The README describes the collection as results at different stages of verification and warns that some of the unformalized results could have issues. Ten families come with abridged summaries of the model’s reasoning, including the irrationality exponent of pi. OpenAI said it consulted the Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence at the Institute for Advanced Study and plans to fund workshops and conferences. The group stated that its role is not an endorsement of how OpenAI obtained the results, and that the mathematical community must assess them.

Reading the proofs. Scott Aaronson of UT Austin wrote on October 7 that the release includes a proof of Subhash Khot’s Unique Games Conjecture, which his wife, the complexity theorist Dana Moshkovitz, has worked toward for her whole career. Lean certificates exist for some of the results but not all. Moshkovitz gave a first reading: the proof invents a recursive code with a noise test that matches neither of the standard codes in the field. She called the paper poorly written and unreadable without AI help. In a later comment Aaronson reported that she now mostly understands the proof and wants to give talks on it.

Aaronson’s list of other results includes integer multiplication in less than n log n time and a positive answer to the unitary synthesis problem that he and Greg Kuperberg posed in 2007. The unitary synthesis paper proves an oracle exists without giving a way to construct it. P versus NP is absent, as are P=BPP and NEXP not in P/poly, which Aaronson read as a sign that the hardest problems remain hard. Asked whether the model could have exploited bugs in Lean, he said he cannot rule it out until the kernel bugs are fixed, but considers it very unlikely.

The reply. The release follows OpenAI’s September 8 Navier-Stokes announcement, in which about 10,000 concurrent agents produced a finite-time singularity for a forced fluid in roughly 88 hours. Po-Shen Loh answered it on September 19 in a guest post on Terence Tao’s blog. He cited the open letters that followed: the Leiden Declaration with more than 4,000 signatories, Math and AI with more than 7,000, and a letter opposing the Caltech Mathathon with more than 2,000.

Loh proposed that every profession wishing to stay human-led adopt a stated axiom, that humans should help humanity flourish. He argues that every point where AI touches banking, water or software needs a domain expert in charge, and expects more such control points than qualified people. The post cites no labor-market figures for that shortfall. For mathematics he lists four changes: end any stigma around using AI for discovery, build a pipeline of people able to oversee AI agents, count teaching in hiring and tenure decisions, and have mathematicians take on government problems.

Aaronson also described two ways of communicating AI results. OpenAI posted undigested proofs and set off a race among humans to explain them. After an Anthropic model supplied the key idea for Virginia Williams and Josh Alman’s 3SUM result, Anthropic let them write the announcement, with compensation.

Labor · Economics · Accounting No V

A model scored
100 where CPAs
averaged 37.

Mercor gave 12 licensed CPAs four month-end close scenarios. Claude Opus 5 ran the same tasks 20 times and scored 100 percent each time. Mercor’s longer benchmark shows a different picture.

Mercor recruited 12 licensed CPAs and gave them four month-end close scenarios drawn from its APEX-Accounting benchmark. Each required digging through a company’s working files, finding the right figures, doing the math and delivering a table of results. The participants averaged about five and a half years of experience and worked without AI assistance. Accuracy ranged from 0 percent to about 90 percent, the average was about 37 percent, and most attempts took 30 to 180 minutes.

Claude Opus 5 ran the same tasks 20 times. It scored 100 percent on every attempt and finished each in under 10 minutes. Mercor puts Opus 5 at $0.21 per rubric criterion met and the accountants at $10.35, a ratio of 49 to 1. The human figure uses the US median accountant wage. The model figure does not appear to include the review time a firm would add.

The setting favored the model. The tasks were simplified items from a benchmark built to stump frontier models, then given to humans with no coworkers to ask and no accumulated job context. They reward detail-oriented instruction following and file search. They do not cover client communication, clarifying questions or tacit knowledge of a company. Mercor says it first designed the study to measure augmentation, then dropped that arm when the model alone reached the ceiling.

The longer benchmark. The 100 percent result applies to medium-length, well-defined tasks. On Mercor’s APEX-Accounting leaderboard, which measures long-horizon work across accounting software, spreadsheets and PDFs, the top entry is Claude Opus 5.5 at 61.8 percent, followed by Fable 5.1 at 61.0 percent. Mercor reports a Pass@1 of 15.4 percent for Opus 5.5, meaning a fully passing first attempt on roughly one task in seven.

Together the two datasets describe a model that is reliable on bounded close steps and inconsistent on the multi-application close a finance team runs at month end. That reading is a commentator’s interpretation, not Mercor’s framing.

Read it as a vendor study. The sample is 12 accountants on four tasks, with Opus 5 contributing 20 attempts. Mercor sells human data and evaluation services and built the benchmark. The full paper sits behind a download link, so this account relies on Mercor’s blog summary and press coverage. The study has not been peer reviewed.

Accounting teams can run their own baseline with their staff, their files and a rubric drawn from real close tasks. Cost comparisons should include review time and the cost of failures on long, cross-application work, since the 49-to-1 ratio covers neither. Task-level results like this one are the kind of evidence The Exponential Replacement Curve weighs against job exposure data.
