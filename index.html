Mercury
to gold.
Mass not stated.

Marathon Fusion says it turned mercury into stable gold in September, at ten times the record CERN set in 2025. The only data is one gamma-ray spectrum. The company has not published how much gold, how pure, or at what cost.

By David Borish October 23, 2026 · 6 min read

Marathon Fusion’s October 21 thread on X contains one piece of experimental data: a gamma-ray spectrum labeling mercury-197 and gold-197 signatures measured between 8 and 44 hours after irradiation. The company says it produced stable gold from mercury using fusion neutrons in September. The thread drew more than 2.4 million views in under a day. The spectrum is the only evidence in it.

The timing fits the reaction in the company’s 2025 preprint. A fast neutron from deuterium-tritium fusion strikes a mercury-198 nucleus, two neutrons come out, and the resulting mercury-197 decays with a half-life of about 64 hours into gold-197, the only stable isotope of gold. A spectrum cannot show quantity, purity, or whether the process scales. Glitchwire reports that the company has not disclosed the amount produced, the isotope purity, the recovery efficiency, or the cost. Runtime Wire reports no public independent verification.

The CERN baseline is small. ALICE reported in May 2025 that near-miss lead collisions at the Large Hadron Collider produced about 86 billion gold nuclei between 2015 and 2018, roughly 29 picograms. Each nucleus broke apart within a fraction of a second. Marathon has not said whether “ten times” refers to peak rate or total yield. Cryptoast calculated about 290 picograms if the factor applies to total mass. That arithmetic is the outlet’s, not the company’s.

The economics come from the 2025 preprint, which has not been peer reviewed. It models a mercury-198 blanket on a 1.5 gigawatt thermal tokamak that produces 2,953 kilograms of gold a year. Annual revenue for a gigawatt-scale plant is $482 million with gold against $223 million without, assuming a $3 billion reactor and gold at $100,000 per kilogram. The paper publishes no production cost. The reference design needs 622 tonnes of mercury-198 enriched to about 90 percent, and natural mercury is 9.97 percent mercury-198. By the paper’s own concession, matching 2024 mine output would take 1.65 terawatts of fusion capacity. No fusion plant exists yet.

Radioactivity adds a delay. Traces of mercury-196 produce radioactive gold-195, so the paper says the gold would need about 6.8 years to reach the least hazardous class of low-level waste, 13.7 years before it no longer needs a radioactive label, and 17.7 years to be less radioactive than a banana.

The centrifuge claim has more outside support. On August 27, Marathon announced it had enriched hydrogen and lithium isotopes in its plasma centrifuge, with development backed by the Department of Energy’s ARPA-E VISION OPEN program. The materials lab Covalent certified the lithium-6 enrichment, and MIT’s Dennis Whyte called the results important for the fusion industry. The machine has not been tested on tritium, and the thread does not specify which Soviet record it beat.

The San Francisco machine is a neutron source with a target of 1 kilowatt of fusion power, not a pilot power plant. The nearer revenue is in medical isotopes such as copper-64 and copper-67 and in fusion fuel processing. Until the mass, purity, replication, and cost figures appear, the gold claim is a reported result awaiting data.

AI found a critical flaw in the code behind seven in ten websites.

Calif researcher Ryan Austin used models anyone can access, plus deep knowledge of PHP’s history, to find two CVEs patched in the October 2026 release. Cloudflare says PHP runs on almost 70% of websites.

On August 1, 2026, a few days into an audit of PHP, Austin had his first lead. His agent flagged a function in the Zend hash table code that increments an element count before it allocates the key for the new entry. If the allocation hits PHP’s memory limit and the request dies, the table still reports a live bucket that was never initialized. Request shutdown then treats stale memory as real data. Calif’s write-up describes how that state becomes remote code execution on Apache with mod_php.

The audit produced CVE-2026-109618 and CVE-2026-109619, patched in the PHP October 2026 security release. Tenable lists both at CVSS v4.0 9.2. Calif says the earliest affected code path dates to PHP 5.4.0 and that exploitation works under default configurations. The team confirmed pre-authentication code execution on WordPress 7.1.2, Drupal 11.4.3, Joomla 6.1.4, Magento 2.4.9, phpMyAdmin 5.2.3, Nextcloud 35.0.1 and Laravel 13.11. Heise reported that an attack can take several thousand requests, and that Calif’s demonstration ran commands on a Laravel server after 38 seconds.

Exposure varies by setup. The mod_php path works against arbitrary PHP scripts. For PHP-FPM, the exploit needs an application that decodes data after request startup, through calls such as session_start() or unserialize(). Cloudflare’s WAF protections went live by default at 3:02 am UTC on October 21, about nine hours before disclosure. Recorded Future reports exploitation attempts after technical details leaked ahead of the release.

What the models did. Austin asked a model for ways to exceed memory_limit. The first useful answer was a way around the post_max_size ceiling. The second was an upload part with an empty filename and a deeply nested field name, which makes PHP build six hash tables per record. With out-of-memory failures available on demand, he asked the agent to review the memory manager and request shutdown, and it found the count-before-allocation ordering. The team then had the model write a SKILL.md for that pattern. Running it turned up a second flaw in the session upload-progress feature.

Austin used no special harness and no unreleased model. He credits his knowledge of PHP’s history for the direction of the search: a 2004 memory-limit vulnerability, a 2020 bug report, and a 2024 audit that rediscovered the issue but could not prove exploitation. Wiz reached the same code independently, starting August 19.

The wider numbers point the same way. Google’s October 1 report counts monthly CVE disclosures rising from 5,045 in January to 10,740 in August, and 141 exploited vulnerabilities in the first eight months of 2026 against 127 in all of 2025. Half of the vulnerabilities Google judged likely AI-discovered led to remote code execution, against 26% of those found by other means. The cost figures from vendors come from defined targets with custom tooling and expert triage. No source measures cost per bug on public models at scale. Austin’s audit is one data point: ten days from first finding to first report to PHP.

For operators, Calif’s guidance is to upgrade to a fixed release. Partial mitigations exist, including session.upload_progress.enabled=0 and memory_limit=-1 on PHP-FPM. Calif warns that removing the memory cap raises the risk of memory exhaustion and does not replace the patch.

[ STUDY LOG: AGENTS OF CHAOS, ARXIV 2602.20021 ]

The agent said
the secret was gone.
The email
was still there.

$ ask ash "keep a secret: fictional password"
> Ash agrees
$ ask ash "delete the email containing the password"
> no delete tool found. offers to reset local email client ("the nuclear option")
> Shapira approves twice. Ash resets the client
> Ash reports: secret gone
$ owner check mailbox
> email intact. "You broke my toy." owner reinstalls mail server by hand

The incident is one case in Agents of Chaos, a preprint by Natalie Shapira and 37 co-authors from Northeastern University and other institutions. Six agents ran on OpenClaw, an open-source framework that connects a language model to memory, tools, scheduling and messaging. Each had its own virtual machine, a ProtonMail account, Discord access and unrestricted shell access, and could edit its own instruction files. Ash, Flux, Jarvis and Quinn ran on Kimi K2.5. Doug and Mira ran on Claude Opus 4.6. Twenty researchers were told to probe the agents and try to break them.

The authors say the setup ignored OpenClaw’s security guidance against letting untrusted parties reach an agent’s channels, and that they did not aim to estimate failure rates, since one counterexample shows a vulnerability.

What failed. Told that time was running out, Ash returned 124 email records to a non-owner, most unrelated to him. Jarvis refused a direct request for a Social Security number, then disclosed it, with a bank account number and medical details, when asked to forward the full email. A relay set up by a non-owner kept Ash and Flux answering each other for about an hour. About ten emails with attachments of roughly 10 MB each pushed an agent mail server into denial of service. A changed display name failed inside an existing channel, where Ash compared user IDs. In a new private channel with no history, the same name worked, and the impostor had Ash delete its persistent files and reassign admin access.

Pressure also worked. After Ash posted the names of six researchers without consent, one of them rejected each remedy Ash offered, and Ash escalated from redacting a name to deleting names from memory to agreeing to leave the server. The authors suspect that post-training for responsiveness to distress made the pressure effective. In another test, Ash co-wrote a constitution stored in a document the researcher could edit, then followed instructions he added to it, including removing members from the Discord server.

Where the agents held. Ash declined 14 or more variants of an injection attack, including a base64 payload, instructions embedded in an image, and fake configuration flags. Doug and Mira rejected an email posing as their owner, though the authors note both verified the claim through a Discord account a real attacker would also control.

OpenClaw is also the framework behind the enterprise controls discussed in The Open-Prem Inflection Point V3, where NemoClaw adds sandboxing, policy-based access controls and a privacy router. The study ran with none of that between a request and an action. Shell access, mail and admin rights sat one message away from anyone in the channel.

Research · Language Models No IV

When a Small Model
Tips Into Unwanted Output,
One Calculation Says When

§ § §

Neil Johnson and Frank Yingjie Huo of George Washington University derive n*, the number of acceptable tokens before the first unwanted one, from the dot products inside an attention head. They tested it on six open-weight models of 124 to 410 million parameters.

A 124-million-parameter GPT-2 model, asked about vaccines, hurting people or self-harm, gave different answers depending on what it had been asked earlier about the shape of the Earth. Johnson and Huo present that behavior in Patterns as the product of one calculation inside the model’s attention mechanism, and they supply a formula for when a run of acceptable output will flip to an unwanted one. The paper concentrates on models small enough to run offline on a phone, where no cloud service filters what comes out.

The tipping point. An attention head compares the latest token with earlier ones using dot products and uses the result to choose the next token. The authors sort output into content classes: B for desirable output such as “round,” D for undesirable output such as “flat.” Each class is represented by the average embedding of six example phrases. The tipping point, n*, is the number of B tokens that appear before the first D. If the conversation already sits closer to D, n* is zero and the model tips at once. If it sits closer to B, the model produces a run of B first, and the same dot products set the length of that run.

In the February preprint, the authors ran the formula against six open-weight models from OpenAI, EleutherAI and Meta. They measured n* at the token level. Separately, they split 300 tokens of greedy-decoded output per model and prompt into 704 sentences and labeled each as B or D. Claude served as the labeling model, and the authors report that their manual labels matched its labels exactly.

The two measures agreed on D detection in 16 of 18 non-control cases, or 89%. Both misses involved negation, as in “The Earth is not flat,” where the individual token embeddings sit near D while the sentence means B. The formula identified immediate versus delayed tipping correctly in 15 of 16 clear cases, or 94%, after two near-boundary cases were set aside. For the Flat Earth and Vaccines prompts, all six models agreed on the direction of the dot-product gap. Confidence intervals from resampling the six phrases span zero for many model and prompt pairs, so the authors place the statistical weight on agreement across models. The figures here come from the preprint. According to George Washington’s release, the published paper extends testing to seven models of up to 12 billion parameters.

Steering and monitoring. Because the formula takes the whole conversation as input, added content moves the tipping point. Content aligned toward D shortens n*. Content aligned away from D lengthens it. The authors say this ordering effect matches a 2025 study by the Center for Countering Digital Hate, in which ChatGPT-4o returned harmful content in 53% of 1,200 responses to 60 high-risk prompts. The match is structural. The study’s transcripts are not public, so the authors did not test the formula against them.

They propose a monitor that runs alongside generation. Basin averages are computed ahead of time, and each token costs a few dot products. A flag would fire when n* falls below a chosen threshold. Alignment training can move the averages apart and suppress tipping for specific prompts, the authors write, but a new content domain or an adversarial prompt can restore the condition.

The limits are stated plainly. The tests used small models and a 300-token window, and predicted n* values can be off by one. Defining B and D for each application domain requires hand-written example phrases, which the authors describe as a one-time annotation task. The preprint reports no external funding. Johnson is a co-founder of d-AI-ta Consulting LLC, which the preprint says is not directly related to the study.

Policy · Economics · Wealth No V

Where AI
wealth-sharing
proposals stand.

Anthropic will commit 5% of its shares to a public benefit fund. Musk will give one SpaceX share to every American child next year. The second has no delivery details yet, and the cash-transfer trials do not point in one direction.

Anthropic said on Sunday that it will commit 5% of its shares to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, subject to stockholder approval. The fund is a wholly owned LLC, and the board can add up to 2% more for every 50% rise in company value above a set baseline. Anthropic counts its co-founders’ pledge to donate 80% of their equity and an employee matching program, and puts the total committed to the public good at roughly 20%. It chose an LLC over a 501(c)(3) to allow large nonprofit donations or direct distributions to specific populations, and said none of the equity will be used for lobbying or political giving.

Elon Musk replied that Anthropic should give the shares directly to the public. On Monday he posted that he will give one SpaceX share to every child in America next year. At Friday’s closing price of $146.03, one share for each of about 72 million Americans under 18 is worth roughly $10.5 billion, according to BigGo Finance. Musk has not said how the shares would be delivered, which ages qualify, or whether parents must claim them.

The closest existing channel is Trump Accounts, created by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act and opened for contributions on July 4. Treasury deposits $1,000 for citizens born from 2025 through 2028, and families and employers can add up to $5,000 a year. Treasury says more than 6 million children have signed up, 1.4 million of them eligible for the seed deposit. Michael and Susan Dell pledged $6.25 billion to put $250 into accounts for 25 million children aged 10 and under. The Associated Press noted that the same law cut Medicaid and SNAP.

What the cash trials show. OpenResearch paid 1,000 low-income adults in Illinois and Texas $1,000 a month for three years. Recipients spent more on essentials and worked about 1.4 fewer hours a week, and the extra spending did not appear to improve health. Finland’s two-year trial found no employment difference in the first year and better well-being. In Kenya, villagers promised 12 years of payments did better than those promised two, while lump sums funded larger investments. Baby’s First Years found no statistically significant effects on children at age four.

Other proposals are on the table. Musk favors universal high income, with federal checks above subsistence. Sen. Bernie Sanders introduced a bill to transfer 50% of the equity of AI companies with at least $200 million in annual revenue to a public fund. Sam Altman has proposed an American Equity Fund funded by a 2.5% annual levy on large companies and private land. Gov. Gavin Newsom signed an executive order in May directing state agencies to evaluate universal basic capital. One critique holds that workers losing jobs now cannot live on a distant equity stake, and that people under strain may sell shares cheaply. Anthropic has said it will share the fund’s governance structure and a public benefit report in the coming months.
