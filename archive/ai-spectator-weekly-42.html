Mathematics · Computer Science · Physics No I

The AI Spectator, October 27, 2026

Three claims.
Three ways
to check them.

OpenAI says its models disproved the Exponential Time Hypothesis, proved the local smoothing conjecture in all dimensions, and proved the existence of Yang-Mills theory and QCD. Each result has a different review process. None is independently verified yet.

By David Borish October 27, 2026

OpenAI has announced three more results from its models, in computer science, mathematics and physics. Everything below is OpenAI’s account of its own work. The announcement is as notable for how it plans to have the results checked as for the results themselves.

The first is a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, proposed by Impagliazzo, Paturi and Zane and commonly stated as the claim that 3-SAT cannot be solved in subexponential time. OpenAI says its models produced a natural-language disproof on October 16, with a Lean formalization the next day. Hundreds of papers on conditional hardness in graph theory, computational biology, databases and computational geometry rest on the hypothesis. A refutation would reopen how fast those problems could be solved, though finding such algorithms would take significant further research.

The second is the local smoothing conjecture, proposed by Christopher Sogge in 1991. Guth, Wang and Zhang proved the two-dimensional case in 2019, and Wang and Zahl proved the three-dimensional Kakeya conjecture in 2025. OpenAI says its proof, built on a novel auxiliary partial differential equation, covers all dimensions and also resolves the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures in all dimensions. The AI output is dated October 18, with a Lean formalization on October 19.

The third is physics. OpenAI says its models produced proofs of the existence of Yang-Mills theory on September 9 and of quantum chromodynamics on September 18, building on Balaban’s constructive quantum field theory work, along with a unified completion of the Standard Model. Pure Yang-Mills existence is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Millennium Prize problems. A Lean formalization of the Yang-Mills proof was finished October 16. The article notes that none is mentioned for the QCD proof.

Each result gets its own release path. The math paper and Lean proof sit in OpenAI’s public GitHub repository. For the Exponential Time Hypothesis, a group led by Impagliazzo, Paturi and Ryan Williams of MIT will verify and explain the result. The group asked that all raw data be made public and that OpenAI fund a conference, and OpenAI agreed to both. For physics, a group led by David Gross, with Michael Douglas, Arthur Jaffe, Igor Klebanov and Nikita Nekrasov, is withholding the AI-produced artifacts and writing up the result itself.

OpenAI describes the manuscripts as raw research data. Participants sign no nondisclosure agreements, are unpaid, and do not speak for OpenAI. OpenAI says it does not seek academic credit for what its models discover, and it acknowledges that choosing the first group leaders creates an information asymmetry. It is giving $5 million to the Simons Foundation for workshops and programs on understanding AI-produced results.

Two gaps remain. No date or location is given for the Exponential Time Hypothesis conference, and no timeline is given for the physics writeup. Until those groups report, these are claims with proofs attached, not settled results.

Open-Source · Infrastructure · Cost No II
V4

Companies start building
the software they used to rent.

The Open-Prem Inflection Point V4 extends the argument from models and hardware to application software. As coding models improve, enterprises build more of their own tools from permissively licensed code and keep the results in-house.

Earlier editions of the Open-Prem Inflection Point argued that companies could own their models, agents and hardware. The V4 paper adds applications. The anchor example is ArtCraft. In early October, developer Brandon Thomas posted to Hacker News that “software is over.” He had used Claude Opus 5.5 to write Rust reimplementations of seven Adobe applications, which he calls clean-room, after an Adobe cancellation fee prompted the project.

Within about a week he added equivalents of Word, Excel and PowerPoint. The suite is free and released under MIT and Apache 2.0. By mid-October its Photoshop equivalent, PhotoCraft, had 64,700 GitHub stars, 11,300 forks, 2,429 commits and more than 1,700 tests. It re-saved 307 of 309 test PSD files with a matching render. Every action is one of more than 500 commands, callable from the interface, a command line, a JSON control channel or an MCP server.

Survey data points the same way. McKinsey’s State of AI 2026, with 1,719 respondents, found 32% of organizations had decided against buying at least one software product or feature because they could build it with agentic coding tools. Among technology companies the figure was 41%. Retool’s 2026 report found 35% of builders had replaced at least one SaaS tool, but its sample is mostly Retool customers and likely overstates the general rate. West Monroe’s chief people officer put the cost of buying equivalents of two internal tools at more than $300,000 a year.

Open-weight models are close on coding. On DeepSWE v1.1, reported by Xiaomi, DeepSeek V4.1-Flash scored 74.2 and MiMo-V2.6-Pro scored 71.9, against 74.0 for both Claude Opus 5 and GPT-6 Astra. Xiaomi compared against Opus 5, not Opus 5.5. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, MiMo-V2.6-Pro leads open-weight models at 46, while Claude Opus 5.5 leads overall at 58.

The cost case depends on the baseline. MiMo-V2.6-Pro’s API costs $0.435 per million input tokens and $0.87 per million output tokens, against $10 and $50 for GPT-6 Astra. In the paper’s example, a $40,000 hardware purchase handling 20 million tokens a day pays back in about 3.3 months against GPT-6 Astra and in about 10 years against MiMo’s hosted API, before power, staff and maintenance.

The paper is direct about limits. One analysis of the Retool data estimates 12.5 to 25 hours of weekly maintenance for a team replacing five SaaS tools. Sixty percent of builders had built something outside IT oversight in the past year, and IBM found shadow AI involved in 43% of breaches, up from 20%. Adobe’s and Microsoft’s terms prohibit studying their software to recreate it, and AI-generated code without a human author is likely not copyright-protected. The paper recommends building internal tools first, piloting AI-built office suites, and keeping systems of record such as ERP, payroll and core banking with vendors.

On October 27, Google DeepMind announced Genie 4, a world model that generates explorable environments in real time. It is the first in the Genie series to produce sound. Genie 3, released as a research preview a little over a year earlier, had none. DeepMind says Genie 4 generates ambient sound, music and character speech together with the video, and that dialogue responds to events and instructions inside the scene.

Users move through a world with keyboard controls and reshape it with typed instructions while it runs. TechCrunch reports edits such as changing the weather or camera angle and adding people or objects. DeepMind says the model simulates aerodynamics and collisions, and that the characters it renders move naturally and can be steered in real time.

The model sits inside a larger Gemini stack. DeepMind says Gemini Omni anchors simulations in real-world appearance and behavior, and other Gemini models provide reasoning. In a robotics experiment, Gemini 4 Argon split tasks into steps and Genie 4 simulated them, including an Apptronik Apollo humanoid playing checkers and a Universal Robots UR5 arm dispensing liquid with a pipette. Google researcher Tejas Kulkarni told reporters he expects hybrid systems, with Gemini writing code and text and Genie simulating the result.

The evidence is thin. The announcement carries no benchmark scores and no side-by-side comparison with Genie 3 or with other companies’ systems, and the demonstrations are Google’s own recordings. DeepMind calls the model early and experimental, and says visual details and object permanence can still shift or drift over time. All Genie 4 video and audio will carry SynthID watermarks.

Access is limited to Google’s preview, and no downloadable weights have been announced. For organizations following the Open-Prem V3 argument, this is a capability to watch but not one that can run on infrastructure they control.

Drug Development · Life Sciences No IV

Six Partners
Report Early Results
from Drug Development with Claude

§ § §

Anthropic’s life sciences team describes work with Generate Biomedicines, Divergence Labs, BigHat Biosciences, Enveda, The Column Group and Bristol Myers Squibb. The results are early, and most are preclinical.

Anthropic’s life sciences team has published a report on six drug development partnerships. The results range from a laboratory finding to a documentation workflow. Anthropic says plainly that the work is early and mostly preclinical, meaning none of the drugs has been tested in people.

Generate Biomedicines. Six scientists used Claude Code to propose new designs for a section of the genetic instructions in a CAR T-cell therapy. One design was about 70 percent shorter than the standard version. In lab tests, immune cells carrying it made roughly ten times as many receptors, killed tumor cells more efficiently and stayed active longer. The shorter section could free room for instructions aimed at solid tumors, which CAR T-cell therapy has historically not treated.

Divergence Labs. The company is developing a food allergy treatment that targets IgE. Claude generated roughly 35,000 antibody designs in about a month. Three bound IgE, and 20 dislodged it from immune cells. Three of those 20 appeared to work better than the standard medication. The combined two-arm molecule has not yet been made or tested.

BigHat Biosciences. With Claude connected to BigHat’s lab data system, the team made and tested about 1,010 antibody variants over roughly ten weeks. Claude and an Anthropic researcher reanalyzed the data and proposed new metrics. The best variants bound more than ten times more tightly than the originals. One lead stayed stable at a temperature 18 degrees Celsius higher, and another clumped more than 100 times more slowly.

Enveda. Manufacturing chemists there spent up to 75 percent of their time on paperwork. Claude reviewed 1,061 pages of test documentation, caught all 11 issues human reviewers found and flagged seven more they missed. Human review time fell by 89 percent, from more than 25 hours to under three. Claude also drafted a regulatory document in four hours, against about three months by hand.

The Column Group and Bristol Myers Squibb. The Column Group is establishing a company called Pleiades to run more and more complex drug programs with Claude. Its first Claude skill, which assesses whether a drug target is worth pursuing, was evaluated on 45 target-disease pairs. Bristol Myers Squibb is using Claude to assess the plan for an early-stage clinical-trial drug and estimate the likelihood of trial success, and the companies plan to expand the collaboration in 2027.

Anthropic’s own program. Anthropic acquired Mithril Medicine, a one-person company developing treatments for two rare forms of muscular dystrophy. Founder Cody Tranbarger used Claude to coordinate contracts, logistics and experiments with 11 external labs. A candidate went from idea to promising mouse results in under 18 months. Anthropic expects one candidate could begin human trials as early as late 2028, if results hold.

What the report does not show. The claims come from Anthropic and its partners, and the drug candidates have not reached patients. Anthropic plans to extend Claude into clinical trials as programs advance, and it is offering Claude Science, credits for life scientists, a verification program for trusted organizations and an open call for guest investigators.

Labor · Economics · Agents No V

Wages move
before the
jobs do.

A Google DeepMind essay by Alex Imas, Andrew Koh and Shane Legg argues that coordination, not competence, decides when AI reorganizes firms. Pay for work AI can match falls first.

The essay, published October 29 on the DeepMind Institute site, starts from a measurement gap. Benchmarks test isolated agents, but economic outcomes are set by competing organizations. A model can beat every human one-on-one and still lose to a well-structured team. The authors want team-versus-team comparisons, with AI teams, human teams and mixed teams.

Their evidence is a quiz. One hundred people answered questions in math, verbal reasoning, science, patterns, culture and economics, and scores in one domain were a weak predictor of scores in another. According to the authors, pairs chosen for complementary strengths beat randomly chosen pairs by 32 percent on a task. AI models are jagged in the same way, and because models resemble one another, adding copies adds capacity but not breadth.

Duplication is the wage mechanism. Human expertise is in fixed supply, so wages rise as firms hire further up the talent distribution. AI can be copied at the cost of compute, and where it matches human capability the authors say human wages tie to that cost. Human coordination is limited by attention, tacit knowledge lost in handoffs and misaligned career incentives. AI agents can share identical weights and full working state, so those limits need not apply. The authors concede that current agents coordinate poorly.

They describe three phases of organizational scaling. Below a coordination floor, solo work beats teams. In the middle, output per person rises with division of labor and then declines as the firm grows. Past what they call the supercooperative threshold, each added member raises productivity, and firm size is limited only by the size of the market. Competence moves the economy gradually, they argue, while coordination moves it in jumps.

Four regimes follow. With minimal AGI, market structure barely changes but wages move first, and employment statistics are weak evidence that AI has had no effect. With supercompetent AI, firms become more productive and leaner, not larger, and routine specialist pay declines. With supercooperative AI, AI-only firms in deep standardized markets such as software, logistics and finance grow and abruptly outcompete smaller ones. With artificial superintelligence, even niche markets lose their shelter, and spending may shift toward relational work such as healthcare, education and craft.

On current conditions the authors point to leaner startups, flatter org charts, softer entry-level hiring and steady demand for experienced people who direct complex work. They recommend benchmarks that score agent organizations on dividing unfamiliar problems without duplication, sharing context across handoffs and catching errors over long horizons. They also recommend economic statistics that track compute spending alongside payroll, and treating wages and job roles as the earliest signals. The figures are the authors’ own and have not been independently checked.
